Chapter 1 : Counting Principles

1.1 Addition Rule (Sum Rule)

Statement : If a task can be done in one of two mutually exclusive ways, where the first way has m outcomes and the second has n outcomes, then the total number of ways is m + n.

The key word is OR : you do either one thing or the other, never both.

Generalised : for k mutually exclusive ways with n₁, n₂, …, nₖ outcomes, the total is n₁ + n₂ + … + nₖ.

Examples

  • Example 1 : A student can pick a project from 5 Maths topics or 7 CS topics. Total choices = 5 + 7 = 12.
  • Example 2 : To travel from Trivandrum to Kochi, you can take 3 trains or 4 buses. Total ways = 3 + 4 = 7.

★ Note ★

If the ways overlap (some outcomes are counted in both), the rule fails and you must use inclusion–exclusion : |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|.

1.2 Multiplication Rule (Product Rule)

Statement : If a task is done in two or more stages, where stage 1 can be done in m ways and, for each of those, stage 2 can be done in n ways, then the total number of ways is m × n.

The key word is AND : you do one thing and then the next.

Generalised : for k stages with n₁, n₂, …, nₖ ways, the total is n₁ × n₂ × … × nₖ.

Examples

  • Example 1 : A menu has 4 starters and 6 main courses. A meal (one starter and one main) can be chosen in 4 × 6 = 24 ways.
  • Example 2 : How many 3-digit numbers can be formed using digits 1 to 9 if repetition is allowed? Each of the 3 positions has 9 choices : 9 × 9 × 9 = 729.
  • Example 3 : Same question without repetition : 9 × 8 × 7 = 504.
  • Example 4 : How many 3-letter strings can be made from the English alphabet (repetition allowed)? 26 × 26 × 26 = 17,576.
RuleKey wordOperation
Addition ruleOR (one thing or the other)add : m + n
Multiplication ruleAND (one thing, then the next)multiply : m × n

1.3 Using both together

Problem : A password has 1 letter (A to Z) followed by either a digit (0 to 9) or a symbol (from 4 symbols). How many passwords are possible?

  • Letter : 26 ways
  • Digit or symbol : 10 + 4 = 14 ways (addition rule)
  • Total : 26 × 14 = 364 (multiplication rule)

Chapter 2 : Permutations (Order Matters)

A permutation is an arrangement of objects in a definite order. Use it when the order of selection changes the outcome (ranks, codes, seating, words).

ⁿPᵣ = n! / (n − r)!

Numbering note : the school notes start the permutation examples at “Example 2”; I have kept their numbering.

Example 2 – Arrangements without repetition

  • 4-digit PINs with no repeated digit : ¹⁰P₄ = 10 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 5040
  • With repetition allowed : 10⁴ = 10,000

Example 3 – Identical objects

  • Arrangements of the letters of BANANA : 6! / (3! 2!) = 720 / 12 = 60 (3 A’s, 2 N’s, 1 B)
  • Arrangements of MISSISSIPPI : 11! / (4! 4! 2!) = 34,650 (4 I’s, 4 S’s, 2 P’s, 1 M)

Example 4 – Circular arrangement

  • 5 people around a round table : (5 − 1)! = 24
  • 6 distinct beads on a necklace : 5! / 2 = 60

Restricted arrangements

  • Items together : treat them as one block, arrange the blocks, then arrange inside the block.
  • Items never together : total arrangements − arrangements with them together.
  • Position conditions : fill the restricted position first, then the rest.

Example 5 – Together and never together

  • 5 people in a row, A and B must sit together : 4! × 2! = 24 × 2 = 48 (block AB + 3 others = 4 objects; AB can be in 2 orders)
  • A and B must not sit together : 5! − 48 = 120 − 48 = 72

Example 6 – Position condition

  • 3-digit numbers from digits 1 to 5, no repetition : ⁵P₃ = 60
  • Even numbers : last digit 2 or 4 (2 ways), then 4 × 3 = 12 for the rest : 2 × 12 = 24

★ Key Observations ★

  • Ask : “does the order matter?” Yes ⇒ permutation.
  • Repeated letters : divide by the factorial of each repeated count.
  • “Together” ⇒ glue into a block; “never together” ⇒ subtract from the total.

Chapter 3 : Combinations (Order Does Not Matter)

A combination is a selection of objects where the order is irrelevant (committees, teams, hands of cards).

ⁿCᵣ = n! / ( r! (n − r)! ) = ⁿPᵣ / r!

Properties

  • ⁿCᵣ = ⁿC₍ₙ₋ᵣ₎
  • ⁿC₀ = ⁿCₙ = 1 and ⁿC₁ = n
  • If ⁿCₓ = ⁿCᵧ, then x = y or x + y = n
  • Selecting r objects from n with repetition allowed : ⁽ⁿ⁺ʳ⁻¹⁾Cᵣ

Example 7 – Basic selection

  • Choose 3 people from 8 : ⁸C₃ = (8 × 7 × 6) / (3 × 2 × 1) = 56
  • Handshakes among 10 people : ¹⁰C₂ = 45

Example 8 – Selection with conditions

  • Committee of 3 men and 2 women from 6 men and 5 women : ⁶C₃ × ⁵C₂ = 20 × 10 = 200
  • Choose 4 from 10 with one particular person always included : ⁹C₃ = 84
  • Same selection with that person excluded : ⁹C₄ = 126

Example 9 – “At least” problems

Committee of 4 from 5 men and 4 women with at least 2 women.

  • Total = ⁹C₄ = 126
  • No women : ⁵C₄ = 5
  • One woman : ⁴C₁ × ⁵C₃ = 4 × 10 = 40
  • At least 2 women = 126 − 5 − 40 = 81

Example 10 – Geometry

  • Diagonals of an n-sided polygon = ⁿC₂ − n (all pairs of vertices minus the n sides)
  • For a hexagon : ⁶C₂ − 6 = 15 − 6 = 9

Distributing identical objects

The number of non-negative integer solutions of x₁ + x₂ + … + xₖ = n is ⁽ⁿ⁺ᵏ⁻¹⁾C₍ₖ₋₁₎.
For positive solutions (each xᵢ ≥ 1) it is ⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾C₍ₖ₋₁₎.

Example 11 – Identical sweets

  • Distribute 10 identical sweets among 3 children : ¹²C₂ = 66
  • If each child must get at least one sweet : ⁹C₂ = 36

Chapter 4 : Practice Problems (Permutations and Combinations)

1. Evaluate ⁸P₃ and ⁸C₃.

⁸P₃ = 8 × 7 × 6 = 336; ⁸C₃ = 336 / 6 = 56

2. In how many ways can 6 people be seated in a row if two of them, A and B, must sit together?

Block AB + 4 others = 5 objects : 5! × 2! = 120 × 2 = 240

3. How many committees of 3 can be chosen from 7 people?

⁷C₃ = 35

4. How many different arrangements of the letters of the word BANANA are there?

6! / (3! 2!) = 60

5. Find the number of diagonals of an octagon.

⁸C₂ − 8 = 28 − 8 = 20

6. A team of 5 is chosen from 6 boys and 4 girls with at least 1 girl. How many teams are possible?

All teams − teams with no girl = ¹⁰C₅ − ⁶C₅ = 252 − 6 = 246

Chapter 5 : Pigeonhole Principle

Definition : If n + 1 or more objects are placed into n boxes, then at least one box contains at least 2 objects.

Generalised PHP : If N objects are placed into k boxes, then at least one box contains at least ⌈N/k⌉ objects.

(⌈x⌉ means “round up to the next whole number”.)

Problems

P1. Show that among any 13 people, at least two were born in the same month.

There are 12 months (boxes) and 13 people (objects), so by PHP two share a month.

P2. A class has 100 students. Show that at least 9 were born in the same month.

⌈100/12⌉ = ⌈8.33…⌉ = 9.

Chapter 6 : Principle of Inclusion–Exclusion (PIE)

To count the elements in a union of sets : add the sizes of the individual sets, subtract the pairwise overlaps, add back the triple overlaps, and so on.

  • Two sets : |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|
  • Three sets : |A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A| + |B| + |C| − |A∩B| − |A∩C| − |B∩C| + |A∩B∩C|

Problems

P1. In a class of 100 students, 60 like Maths, 50 like CS, and 30 like both. How many like neither?

  • |M ∪ C| = 60 + 50 − 30 = 80
  • Neither = 100 − 80 = 20

P2. How many integers from 1 to 100 are divisible by 2, 3 or 5?

  • |A₂| = 50, |A₃| = 33, |A₅| = 20
  • |A₂∩A₃| = ⌊100/6⌋ = 16, |A₂∩A₅| = 10, |A₃∩A₅| = 6
  • |A₂∩A₃∩A₅| = ⌊100/30⌋ = 3
  • Total = 50 + 33 + 20 − 16 − 10 − 6 + 3 = 74. So 100 − 74 = 26 are divisible by none of them.

(⌊x⌋ means “round down”. The school notes write the 100/2 and 100/5 counts directly.)

P3. How many integers from 1 to 1000 are divisible by neither 5 nor 7?

  • ⌊1000/5⌋ = 200, ⌊1000/7⌋ = 142, ⌊1000/35⌋ = 28
  • Divisible by 5 or 7 = 200 + 142 − 28 = 314
  • Answer = 1000 − 314 = 686

P4. In a survey of 200 people, 120 read newspaper A, 90 read B, 70 read C, 40 read A and B, 30 read A and C, 25 read B and C, and 15 read all three. How many read at least one?

  • 120 + 90 + 70 − 40 − 30 − 25 + 15 = 200. So everyone reads at least one.

Chapter 7 : Recurrence Relations

A recurrence relation is an equation that defines each term of a sequence using one or more of the preceding terms. To pin down a specific sequence, you also need initial conditions (the starting values).

Example : Fibonacci series

  • Recurrence relation : F(n) = F(n − 1) + F(n − 2), for n ≥ 2
  • Initial conditions : F(0) = 0, F(1) = 1
  • First eight terms : 0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13

Each new term is the sum of the previous two terms (for example F(7) = F(6) + F(5) = 8 + 5 = 13).

Chapter 8 : Euler Path and Euler Circuit

Definition. An Euler path (or Euler trail) is a walk in a graph that traverses every edge exactly once. It may start and end at different vertices. Vertices may be repeated, but no edge can be repeated.

Example 1 : Simple path graph

  • Edges : AB, BC, CD
  • Euler path : A → B → C → D
  • Explanation : the path traverses AB, BC and CD exactly once. It starts at A and ends at D, so it is an Euler path but not an Euler circuit.
  • Vertex degrees : deg(A) = 1, deg(B) = 2, deg(C) = 2, deg(D) = 1. Exactly two vertices (A and D) have odd degree.

Example 2 : Square with diagonal AC

  • Edges : AB, BC, CD, DA, AC
  • Euler path : A → B → C → D → A → C (uses all 5 edges once)
  • Degrees : deg(A) = 3, deg(B) = 2, deg(C) = 3, deg(D) = 2 – exactly two odd vertices (A and C), and the path starts at A and ends at C.

The school PDF prints this path as “A → B → C → D → A →” (the last vertex is cut off). Completed above as … → A → C.

Euler Circuit

An Euler circuit is a closed trail in a connected graph that traverses every edge exactly once and returns to the starting vertex.

Condition : a connected graph has an Euler circuit if and only if every vertex has even degree.

Euler circuit – examples

  • Graph G₁ : V = {A, B, C, D}, E = {AB, BC, CD, DA}
  • Euler circuit : A → B → C → D → A
  • Degrees : deg(A) = deg(B) = deg(C) = deg(D) = 2 (all even)
  • Every edge is traversed exactly once and the circuit returns to A. Hence G₁ has an Euler circuit.
  • Graph G₂ : V = {A, B, C}, E = {AB, BC, CA}
  • Euler circuit : A → B → C → A
  • Degrees : deg(A) = deg(B) = deg(C) = 2 (all even)
  • Hence G₂ has an Euler circuit.
  • Graph G₃ : V = {A, B, C, D}, E = {AB, BC, CD, DA, AC, BD}
  • Degrees : deg(A) = deg(B) = deg(C) = deg(D) = 3 (all odd)
  • An Euler circuit needs every vertex to have even degree. Hence G₃ does not have an Euler circuit.

★ Key Observations ★

  • Euler circuit : connected and all degrees even.
  • In the examples, an Euler path that is not a circuit had exactly two odd-degree vertices – it starts at one and ends at the other.
  • Euler = edges exactly once (vertices may repeat).

Chapter 9 : Hamilton Path and Hamilton Circuit

WordMeaning
PathA trip from one vertex to another along edges.
CircuitA trip that starts and ends at the same vertex.
Simple path / circuitOne that does not repeat any edge.

Hamilton path : a simple path that passes through every vertex exactly once. It starts and ends at different vertices.

Hamilton circuit : a simple circuit that passes through every vertex exactly once and returns to the starting vertex.

  • You do not have to use every edge.
  • Only the start vertex appears twice in a circuit (first and last).
  • If a graph has a Hamilton circuit, it also has a Hamilton path : stop before the final step back.
  • A graph can have a Hamilton path without a circuit.
EulerHamilton
Must cover every …Edge exactly onceVertex exactly once
Repeats allowedVertices can repeatVertices cannot repeat
Open / closedEuler path / Euler circuitHamilton path / Hamilton circuit

Examples

(a) No circuit, but a Hamilton path

  • Path : a → b → c → d. It uses edges ab, bc, cd and visits all 4 vertices once.
  • Why no circuit : vertex a touches only one edge (a–b). A circuit must enter and leave every vertex by two different edges, so a degree-1 vertex rules out a circuit.

(b) Hamilton circuit

  • Circuit : a → b → c → d → e → a. The edges ab, bc, cd, de, ea all exist, every vertex is used once, and we return to a.
  • So it also has a path : a → b → c → d → e.
  • The extra diagonal edges are not needed.

(c) Neither

  • Degree-1 vertices : a, d and f each touch only one edge.
  • A Hamilton path has only two ends, and every degree-1 vertex must be an end. Three such vertices make a path impossible, so a circuit is impossible too.

(d) Hamilton circuit

  • Circuit : a → b → c → d → e → a. The edges ab, bc, cd, de, ea all exist.
  • So it also has a path : a → b → c → d → e.
  • The edges bd and be are not used, and that is fine.

(e) No circuit, but a Hamilton path

In the school PDF, example (e) is only a heading – its figure and explanation are missing. When you have the figure, send it and I will add it.

★ Key Observations ★

  • Degree-1 vertex ⇒ no Hamilton circuit (a circuit needs two edges at every vertex).
  • Three or more degree-1 vertices ⇒ no Hamilton path either.
  • Hamilton circuit ⇒ Hamilton path (drop the last edge). The converse is false.

Chapter 10 : Graph Practice Question

Q1. A graph G has vertex set {A, B, C, D, E} and edges AB, AC, BC, CD, CE, DE. (i) Write its adjacency matrix. (ii) Determine, with justification, whether G has an Euler circuit. (iii) Find a Hamiltonian path in G.

(i) Adjacency matrix

Taking the vertex order A, B, C, D, E (entry 1 if the two vertices are joined by an edge, otherwise 0) :

ABCDE
A01100
B10100
C11011
D00101
E00110

The matrix is symmetric (an undirected graph) and has 0 on the diagonal (no loops).

(ii) Euler circuit?

  • deg(A) = 2, deg(B) = 2, deg(C) = 4, deg(D) = 2, deg(E) = 2 (each degree = sum of that row of the matrix)
  • A connected graph has an Euler circuit if and only if every vertex has even degree.
  • G is connected and all its vertices have even degree, so G has an Euler circuit.
  • Example circuit (extra) : A → B → C → D → E → C → A.

(iii) Hamiltonian path

  • Hamiltonian path : A → B → C → D → E
  • The edges AB, BC, CD and DE belong to G, and the path visits all five vertices exactly once. Hence it is a Hamiltonian path.

★ Method ★

Adjacency matrix : 1 if joined→Degrees = row sums→All even & connected ⇒ Euler circuit→Hamilton path : visit every vertex once

★ Exam Points ★

  • Addition rule = “or”, multiplication rule = “and”. Password example : 26 × (10 + 4) = 364.
  • ⁿPᵣ = n!/(n − r)! ; ⁿCᵣ = n!/(r!(n − r)!) ; ⁿPᵣ = r! × ⁿCᵣ ; ⁿCᵣ = ⁿC₍ₙ₋ᵣ₎.
  • Identical objects : n!/(p! q! r!). Circle : (n − 1)!. Necklace : (n − 1)!/2. Together : block × inside; never together : total − together.
  • At least problems : total − (cases that fail). Diagonals of an n-gon : ⁿC₂ − n.
  • Identical sweets : non-negative solutions ⁽ⁿ⁺ᵏ⁻¹⁾C₍ₖ₋₁₎; each at least 1 : ⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾C₍ₖ₋₁₎.
  • PHP : n + 1 objects in n boxes ⇒ some box has ≥ 2. Generalised : ⌈N/k⌉.
  • PIE (3 sets) : singles − pairs + triple. Neither = total − union.
  • Recurrence : Fibonacci F(n) = F(n−1) + F(n−2), F(0) = 0, F(1) = 1.
  • Euler circuit ⇔ connected and every vertex has even degree. Euler path (not circuit) : two odd vertices.
  • Hamilton : every vertex exactly once. Degree-1 vertex ⇒ no Hamilton circuit; three degree-1 vertices ⇒ no Hamilton path.
